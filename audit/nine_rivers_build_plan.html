Track 2 · your priority

Why the premium tiles don't look premium

I read tile_view.gd rather than guessing. The problem isn't taste — it's four specific technical facts, and each one has a fix.

What the code doesWhy it reads as cheap
Tiles are drawn at a fixed 64 × 84 logical pixels, then camera-scaledYour fine detail — a 1.2px highlight, a 1px rim, a 6px gold band — lands on roughly one physical pixel on a 1080p phone. It turns to mush.
Every surface is a flat StyleBoxFlat fillNo gradient, no texture, no variation across the face. Gold without a gradient is just yellow. This is the single biggest cause.
3D is one offset slab plus a drop shadowA real tile has a curved bevel that catches light along its top edge. A flat rectangle sitting on another flat rectangle never will.
Themes differ by colour and vector ornament onlyJade, gold, obsidian and porcelain are physically different materials. Recolouring the same flat shape can't express that.
Proposal · first pass

Render everything in Blender

  • Model the tile, apply real materials per theme, render each of the 42 tile faces × 4 themes as finished sprites.
  • Swap _draw() for a Sprite2D per tile.
  • Cell-fracture the tile in Blender and render a break animation flipbook per theme.
Arguing against it

Four reasons that version fails

  • 168 sprites. 42 faces × 4 themes, and every new theme costs another 42. A fifth theme becomes a day of rendering instead of a colour change.
  • State explosion. Tiles are also free / blocked / selected / wild / frozen / glass. Baking those in multiplies 168 by six.
  • Symbols get worse, not better. Your vector 梅 and 東 are crisp at any zoom for zero bytes. Baking them into a sprite fixes them at one resolution — a downgrade.
  • Flipbooks are huge and stiff. A per-theme break animation is megabytes, and every tile shatters identically. Players notice by level 20.
Proposal · second pass · build this

Blender renders the material, not the picture

  • Four body sprites, not 168. Blender renders the blank tile body per theme — no symbol, no state — at 256 × 336 (4× the logical size, so it stays sharp on any phone). One albedo plus one normal map each. Eight textures total.
  • A shader does the lighting. A canvas shader reads the normal map against a fixed art-directed light direction. That gives a real curved bevel and per-material response — anisotropic sheen on the gold, soft subsurface on the porcelain, hard specular on the obsidian — with no Light2D in the scene, which matters on the gl_compatibility renderer you ship.
  • Symbols stay vector. Drawn on top exactly as now. Crisp at every zoom, free, and already written.
  • States stay code. Free / blocked / wild / frozen / glass remain shader parameters and overlays. A new state costs a uniform, not a render.
  • Breaking uses real shards. Cell-fracture one tile in Blender, export the 16–20 shard shapes as a single atlas. Godot spawns them with their own velocity, spin and gravity, tinted and normal-lit per theme. Every shatter is different, it costs one small texture, and it layers over the dust particles in tile_shatter_dust.gd you already have.

Why this is the right shape: Blender does the thing code is bad at — real material and real bevel light — and code keeps doing what it's already good at: sharp symbols, cheap states, varied motion. Adding a fifth theme later costs two textures, not forty-two.

Cost: roughly 2–4 MB compressed. You currently ship zero texture assets, and ETC2/ASTC compression is already enabled, so there's room.

The real risk is art direction, not technology. "Premium" is a judgement call, so the plan is to render one tile, put it in front of you, and iterate — not to render four themes and hope.

Track 1 · biggest, and release-blocking

Getting to 1,000 levels that stay interesting

You chose ~100 shapes × goals and modifiers. That's the right call, and it's how large Mahjong titles actually ship. But there's a wall in the way first.

Layouts are hardcoded GDScript. Each of your 11 shapes is a match branch calling _grid() with rectangle bounds. Nobody authors 100 shapes that way, let alone tunes them. Everything else in this track depends on fixing that first.

One good piece of news: _add_pos() already accepts arbitrary coordinates, so real silhouettes — a dragon, a lotus, a character — are possible in the engine today. They're simply never used.

Proposal · first pass

A JSON file per layout, and 1,000 stage entries

  • Convert each layout to JSON coordinates, author ~100 more.
  • Write a table of 1,000 stages pairing a layout with a goal and a modifier.
Arguing against it

Where that comes apart

  • JSON coordinates are unauthorable. Nobody can look at 400 {x,y,z} triples and see a lotus. You can't eyeball it, can't review it, can't fix it.
  • 100 shapes is still 100 shapes. Changing the file format doesn't make the authoring work disappear.
  • 1,000 hand-written stage rows is a spreadsheet, not a difficulty curve. It'd be tuned by guesswork.
  • Nothing proves they're playable. Every board must be solvable and appropriately hard. Hand-checking 1,000 is impossible; shipping them unchecked is worse.
Proposal · second pass · build this

Draw the shapes, generate the stages, let the bot prove them

  • Layouts as ASCII layer maps. One text file per shape, one block per Z-layer, X for a tile. You can see the silhouette while editing it, diff it in git, and fix it in a text editor. Authoring a shape drops from an hour to minutes.
  • Mirror tooling. Most Mahjong layouts are symmetric. Author a quarter or half, let the tool mirror it — cutting the real work to roughly 30 hand-drawn shapes plus variants.
  • Stages are generated, not typed. A stage is layout × tile count × objective × modifier × tile mix. Generate the 1,000 from curve rules, so difficulty is a tuned function rather than a thousand guesses.
  • The bot becomes the validator. You already own this. bot_runner.gd plays real boards through real click handling. Pointed at all 1,000 stages it reports solvability, dead-end rate, taps and achievable flow — then auto-rejects the broken and auto-ranks the rest by measured difficulty. Nobody hand-checks anything.
  • Worlds every ~50 levels. New pond theme, new mechanic, new layout family. Novelty spikes are what carry players past level 100.

The bot is the unlock. The work we did on save integrity accidentally built the tool this track needs. Content at this scale is a validation problem, and you already have the validator.

One honest caution. 1,000 levels before launch is a large content commitment — realistically months, mostly authoring and tuning rather than code. That's your call and I'll plan for it, but it should be a decision you make on purpose, not a number that quietly sets your release date.

Track 3 · roughly a day

Reclaiming the wasted screen space

You were right, and the cause is three lines in camera_controller.gd. Framing assumes a hardcoded 190px top HUD and 230px bottom deck, adds fixed padding, then fits with a single 0.95 factor on whichever axis is tighter.

So a wide, short board like Stone Keep (12 columns, 5 rows) fits to width and leaves a band of dead space above and below. A tall, narrow board wastes it at the sides. Every layout gets the same compromise.

The fix, in order:

  • Measure the HUD instead of assuming it. Read the actual control rects at runtime, so the numbers can't drift out of sync with the UI — and so notches and gesture bars are handled properly.
  • Fit per axis, not per board. Choose the limiting axis, then spend the slack on the other one instead of scaling both down.
  • Framing hints in the layout data. A wide shape can declare it prefers to sit high; a tall one can ask for tighter side margins. One line per layout, authored alongside the shape.
  • Keep the camera locked. Pan and zoom are deliberately disabled for touch accuracy — correct decision, and it means the first framing has to be right.
Track 4

UI, Sub-UI and the frosted look

The structure you want mostly exists — modal_controller.gd already does sub-screens with a back stack, and level select already scrolls. The gap is finish, not architecture.

  • Frosted glass, the cheap way. Real backdrop blur means a BackBufferCopy plus a multi-tap blur every frame — genuinely expensive on budget Android, which is your market. A pre-blurred translucent panel with fine noise and a bright 1px top edge reads as frosted glass at a fraction of the cost. Use real blur only behind full-screen modals, where nothing animates underneath.
  • Icons from the same Blender pipeline. Rendering the koi, lotus and lantern icons as small 3D objects under the same light direction as the tiles is what will make the game feel art-directed rather than assembled. Consistency of lighting is what sells "premium".
  • Scroll containers that feel native. Momentum, overscroll and snap — the difference between "a menu" and "a product".
  • One spacing scale. Current modals use ad-hoc pixel values. A single scale applied everywhere is invisible individually and obvious in aggregate.
